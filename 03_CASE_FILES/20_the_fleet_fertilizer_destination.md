## 20.2 THE KOBELCO FLEET — 17 furnaces, 5 recovering, one unit rate

**Sources:** gov-online.go.jp (Oct 2022, the Heisei Library article
"Energy Plant that Recovers Gold and Silver from Waste"); kobelco-eco.co.jp
facility lists; the Kobelco IR (FY2021 Sagamihara recovery).

| element of the fleet | the record |
|---|---|
| **the fleet size** | *"gold and silver recovery has already commenced at **five out of seventeen locations** around Japan where the company's fluidized bed gasification and melting furnaces have been installed"* |
| **the flagship** | **Sagamihara City Minami Waste to Energy & Recovery Plant** — **525 t/day (175 t/24h × 3 lines)**, 10,000 kW, completed **March 2010**; "the largest Fluidized-bed Gasification and Melting plant in Japan" (Kobelco's own words) |
| **the other melting-furnace plants (named)** | Sennan Clean Center (200 t/d, 2017) · Kofu Kyoto Clean Center (369 t/d, 2017) · Kamiina Clean Center (118 t/d, 2019) · + the rest of the 17 |
| **the physics** | the fluidized bed gasifies waste in circulating sand at 500–550°C; the melting furnace melts the ash → vitrified material (slag); **"the gold and silver remained concentrated in the sand accumulated at the bottom of the fluidized bed furnace"** |
| **the grade, their own comparison** | *"Typically, the gold content of gold ore mined from a gold mine is about **3–5 grams per ton of ore**"* — vs the furnace sand's "tens to thousands of mg/kg" (**Sagamihara: ~6,000 g/t = 1,200–2,000× gold-ore grade**) |
| **the FY2021 unit result** | Sagamihara: **15,427 g Au + 15,856 g Ag** → their IR's own value: **¥100M gold + ¥1.5M silver** |
| **the expansion** | *"Technological development is ongoing to recover other types of precious metals such as platinum, palladium and copper"* |

**The fleet envelope (at their own unit rate):**

| scenario | the arithmetic |
|---|---|
| 5 recovering sites × the Sagamihara unit rate | 5 × ¥100M = **¥500M/yr ≈ $3.4M/yr** (gold only) |
| all 17 sites × the Sagamihara unit rate | 17 × ¥100M = **¥1.7B/yr ≈ $11.6M/yr** (gold only) |
| the throughput basis | Sagamihara alone: 525 t/day × 365 = **~191,625 t/yr** of waste through one plant — the sand concentrate is a fraction of that, unweighed in any public document |

**The read:** the fleet is **17 furnaces, five already recovering**
(their own count), at a unit rate the company itself published
(¥100M/yr at one site). The envelope at their own rate is
**¥500M–¥1.7B per year in gold alone** — booked in no segment, in
any year, in any filing. The Kobelco P&L split (file 12) stands:
the three columns (waste/water/chemical) have no fourth one.

## 20.4 GREEN LANE LANDFILL — the destination record (Toronto's ash, bound)

**Sources:** City of Toronto reports (2023, 2024, 2025); the 1998 and
2006 ECA approvals (ontario.ca); the Auditor General's 2018 audit;
the Solid Waste Management Facilities page.

| element of the destination | the record |
|---|---|
| the site | **Green Lane Landfill**, Township of Southwold, ~200 km from Toronto, just outside London, ON; operating since **1978**; **purchased by the City April 2, 2007** |
| **the tonnage** | **~450,000 t/yr** (2023 report); **418,000 t in 2024** ("including garbage that was brought directly to the landfill from surrounding local communities"); ~420,000 t in 2023; the Auditor General: **740,000 t (2011) → 495,000 t (2017)** — the decline, audited |
| **the capacity** | ECA (1998): max 350,000 m³/yr air space, 5,850,000 m³ total; optimization approval (2006): **+10,900,000 m³**; only Ontario-generated waste; first priority to Elgin/Middlesex/St. Thomas; **full capacity expected ~2035**; Ontario-wide landfill capacity "expected to reach full capacity within ten years" |
| **the contracts** | the Auditor General (2018): *"Management of Contracts Needs Improvement"* — **value of contracts reviewed $265M**; 250 loads/week from City transfer stations; **2,849 of 90,000 loads (3.2%) overweight** since 2011; contingency capacity 325,000 t/yr contracted |
| **the neighbors** | **three First Nations communities: the Chippewas of the Thames, the Munsee Delaware Nation, the Oneida Nation of the Thames** — and the RNG project carries a **First Nations Engagement Grant Fund** |
| **the gas** | landfill gas currently **flared** (Ontario regulation); since 2020 the City works to upgrade it to RNG |
| **the ash, bound** | Highland Creek's ~45,000 t of ash (file 15) enters this tonnage — **the metals inside it, unanalyzed, inside the 418,000 t** |
| **the waste hierarchy, their own diagram** | Toronto's Waste Strategy panels: *"Garbage left after reduction and diversion efforts → Collection and transportation → **Metal recovery and recycling** → Ash disposed in a landfill"* — **the "metal recovery and recycling" step exists in the City's own diagram and is not applied to the ash** |

**The read:** the destination ledger is complete — 418,000 t/yr
entering a hole in the ground with a 2035 expiry, under a $265M
contract audit that found 3.2% overweight loads and "needs
improvement" management, beside three First Nations communities, with
the gas flared and the metals unweighed. **The City's own waste
hierarchy has a "metal recovery" step — and the ash skips it.** The
XRF's municipal case study closes: the grit at the front (1,570 t/yr
Toronto, ~4,000 t/yr DC Water), the cake in the middle (Cu 672/555,
Zn 670/427 mg/kg), the ash at the back (~45,000 t, landfill-bound) —
**three streams, one measurement gap, one instrument.**
