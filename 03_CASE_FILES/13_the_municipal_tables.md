## 13.1 THE ECHIDNA/WERRIBEE PRECEDENT — the full record (the paper)

**Reeves, Plimer & Foster (1999), *Journal of Geochemical Exploration*
65(2):141–153** — "Exploitation of gold in a historic sewage sludge
stockpile, Werribee, Australia: resource evaluation, chemical extraction
and subsequent utilisation of sludge" (cited by 14):

- **1995: Echidna Mining** (Australian gold exploration company)
  **acquired the exploration rights to the ground surrounding Melbourne's
  historic sludge reserves** and "commenced a program of resource
  evaluation, utilising **RNAA, INAA, GFAAS, ICP–MS and FLAAS** to
  determine **31 elements, including Au, Ag, Sb, As, Cd, Hg, Zn, Cu,
  and Pb**."
- The stockpile: **Melbourne's sewage, 1898–1980**, in large closed
  lagoonal tanks at Werribee (Melbourne Water's Western Treatment
  Plant — processes **>500 megalitres/day**, more than half the city's
  raw sewage and industrial waste).
- The study's stated purpose: "to determine Au, Ag and other metal
  variations in both space and time and to investigate the economics of
  chemical extraction of the precious metals."
- The paper's thesis (verbatim): *"Sewage sludges… may be considered
  targets for economic extraction of Au because of the documented Au
  content of sewage sludges worldwide which are of the order of some
  ore deposits currently mined for Au."*

**The read:** a serious five-method resource evaluation (neutron
activation, graphite furnace AAS, ICP-MS, flame AAS) of a city's
82 years of sewage — by a mining company, in 1995, published in a
peer-reviewed journal. The precedent is complete: the right was
acquired, the methods were run, the paper exists. What happened to
the rights after 1999 is the case's open thread (queued §12.3).

## 13.2 TORONTO — THE METALS-TO-WATER LEDGER (NPRI, 2013–2023)

**Environment and Climate Change Canada, National Pollutant Release
Inventory (NPRI ID 0000002240) — City of Toronto, Ashbridges Bay

| 2013 | 7.82 | 576 | 115 | 150 |
| 2014 | 6.99 | 582 | 116 | 699 |
| 2015 | 6.38 | 532 | 213 | 780 |
| 2016 | 6.04 | 622 | 402 | 1,010 |
| 2017 | 10.4 | 602 | 482 | 1,200 |
| 2018 | 10.3 | 617 | 412 | 1,030 |
| 2019 | 11.7 | 584 | 467 | 1,170 |
| 2020 | 10.2 | 509 | 407 | 1,020 |
| 2021 | 9.36 | 618 | 375 | 936 |
| 2022 | 10.4 | 520 | 416 | 1,040 |
| 2023 | 11.13 | 556.56 | 445.25 | 1,113.13 |

**The read:** the NPRI — Canada's own national release inventory —
records **~600 kg of lead, ~1,000 kg of arsenic, ~400 kg of cadmium,
and ~10 kg of mercury** released to water by ONE Toronto plant per
year, 11 years running. These are the REGULATED metals (the ones that
must be reported). The UNREGULATED value — the gold, silver, PGMs,
rare earths — flows the same pipes, is measured by the same Appendix E
and Appendix F, and is never multiplied into a ledger line. The
regulated metals are the cage's own disclosure; the value stream beside
them is the unbooked one.

**Toronto's biosolids tonnage (their own filings):**
- Ashbridges Bay: **~40,000 dry tonnes/yr** biosolids (incl. Humber +
  North Toronto transfers); 147,182 wet tonnes in 2024 @ 26.05% TS;
  Highland Creek incinerates ~10,000 dry tonnes/yr.
- Humber transfers: **54.5–69.9 dry tonnes/day** of biosolids to
  Ashbridges (2023–2025 annual reports).
- **CEC "Taking Stock" (2014–2018):** Toronto's Ashbridges Bay drove
  the increase in Canadian sewage-sector transfers to land application;
  **before 2017, the facility transferred its waste to landfills or
  surface impoundments — including to a site located in the United
  States.** The international waste-movement line: the stream crossed
  a border before it crossed a ledger.

**Toronto's contracts (City of Toronto, Oct 2024):** Lystek
$1,385,832 + Terratec $2,409,570 = **$3.8M/yr to move the stream** —
the cost of moving the mass is booked; the value of the mass is not.

**DC Water's own note:** *"one would have to consume nearly 50 lbs in
a year to get the RDV of zinc… In all cases but that of nickel, the
concentrations of these heavy metals are higher in Centrum vitamins
than in the Bloom product."*

**The read:** DC Water published its own metals table, compared it to
multivitamins, and concluded the biosolids are safe. What the table
does NOT say: the ASU standard's $280/ton (or the EST follow-up's
$480/dry ton) values those same streams carry — the precious metals
(Au, Ag, Pd, Ir, Pt) that the table doesn't list because they aren't
REGULATED. The regulated six are the cage's disclosure; the 13 most
lucrative elements (Ag, Cu, Au, P, Fe, Pd, Mn, Zn, Ir, Al, Cd, Ti, Ga,
Cr) are the unbooked stream. DC Water books Bloom fertilizer at $13.6M
and books nothing for the metals in the same material.

**DC Water tonnage (their own reports):** biosolids hauling 432–1,260
wet tons/day (2006–2016); reuse cost $39.98/wet ton (Oct 2016) —
the cost of moving the mass is booked; the value of the mass is not.

## 13.4 THE US NATIONAL FRAME (the USGS standard)

- **USGS (Kathleen Smith, ACS Denver 2015):** "the gold we found was
  at the level of a **minimal mineral deposit**… might be commercially
  viable to mine it." 16,500 US WWTPs → **~7 million dry tons/yr**
  biosolids nationwide.
- **ASU/Westerhoff (EST 2015):** $13M/yr metals per million people;
  **$280/ton** sludge; **$480/dry ton** in the EST follow-up; $2.6M/yr
  is gold+silver; 20% of the value.
- **USGS 8-year study:** monthly sampling of one plant's biosolids —
  28 mg Ag, 638 mg Cu, 49 mg V, <1 mg Au per kg of waste; the same
  concentrations as the ASU national average, at one plant, for eight
  years.

**The read:** the US national standard (USGS) and the US academic
standard (ASU) both say the same thing at different scales: the stream
is a mineral deposit. The municipalities publish the regulated metals;
the researchers publish the value; nobody multiplies them into a
ledger line.
